RNAi (definition)
//
A biological process in which RNA molecules inhibit gene expression, typically by destruction of specific mRNA molecules or inhibition of translation, reducing protein yield of a specific target mRNA

Natural biological functions of RNAi
//
(1) Protect the genome against invasion by mobile genetic elements such as viruses and transposons; (2) support the developmental programs of eukaryotic organisms
<!--SR:!2026-08-25,3,250-->

1990 — Napoli & Jorgensen: organism and finding
//
Petunia; introduced extra chalcone synthase (CHS) gene expecting deeper violet color, but pigmentation was ~50-fold lower than wild type — the transgene co-suppressed the endogenous CHS gene

1992 — Romano & Macino: organism and finding
//
Neurospora crassa (fungus); observed the same silencing phenomenon, later termed quelling

Guo & Kemphues experiment
//
Injected par-1 mRNA (a putative Ser/Thr kinase) into C. elegans — both sense AND antisense RNA led to degradation of the par message

1998 — Fire & Mello finding
//
Showed the true trigger of RNAi is double-stranded RNA (dsRNA); awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology/Medicine, 2006

How did Fire & Mello prove dsRNA is the true trigger?
//
Highly purified sense and antisense ssRNA were far less effective (sense ~10×, antisense ~100× weaker) than dsRNA against the same mRNA; injecting sense+antisense together was effective — showing hybridization to dsRNA triggers silencing

Forward genetics
//
Starts from a phenotype (mutant organism) and proceeds to the gene that encodes it

Reverse genetics
//
Starts from a genotype (DNA sequence) and proceeds to the phenotype, by altering the sequence or inhibiting its expression

Why is RNAi called a reverse-genetics tool?
//
Because the RNAi pathway can be exploited to knock down a chosen protein and reveal its effect on phenotype



Why do bacteria use CRISPR instead of RNAi?
//
Bacteria lack a directly homologous RNAi machinery; instead they use CRISPR as their own adaptive immune system to recognize invading DNA/RNA and eliminate it

tracrRNA (function)
//
Combines with mature crRNA to activate and guide the Cas9 nuclease to the correct DNA target

CRISPR stage 1: Adaptation (aka Immunization)
//
A short viral DNA fragment is cleaved and integrated into the CRISPR array as a new spacer (memory of infection)

CRISPR stage 2: Expression (aka Biogenesis)
//
The array is transcribed into pre-crRNA; processing (with tracrRNA + RNase III) yields mature crRNAs bound to Cas protein

CRISPR stage 3: Interference (aka Silencing)

The crRNA–Cas complex seeks out and cleaves complementary double-stranded viral DNA

Step-by-step Cas9 cutting mechanism
//
crRNA+tracrRNA+Cas9 form a complex → scans DNA for a PAM (NGG) → if present, DNA unwinds → crRNA base-pairs the target sequence upstream of the PAM → Cas9's HNH domain cuts one strand, RuvC cuts the other → double-strand break (DSB)

How do crRNAs differ from miRNA/siRNA?
//
crRNAs are transcribed from stored viral DNA fragments (spacers) and associate with Cas proteins to target double-stranded DNA — not single-stranded RNA as miRNA/siRNA do

Three steps of the core small-RNA silencing pathway
//
Biogenesis → RISC loading → Silencing

Step 1 of the core RNAi pathway (Dicing)
//
A large double-stranded RNA is diced by Dicer into small double-stranded interfering RNAs, 21–28 bp long

Step 2 of the core RNAi pathway (Assembly)
//
The small interfering RNAs and proteins assemble into ribonucleoprotein particles

Step 3 of the core RNAi pathway (Unwinding)
//
The duplex is unwound to produce the single-stranded RNA-Induced Silencing Complex (RISC)

Step 4 of the core RNAi pathway (Targeting)
//
RISC targets an mRNA sequence complementary to its interfering RNA

Step 5 of the core RNAi pathway (Base-pairing)
//
The RISC guide base-pairs with its target in the mRNA

Outcome of perfect RISC-target pairing (step 6A)
//
mRNA is cleaved and degraded (siRNA-type outcome)

Outcome of imperfect RISC-target pairing (step 6B)
//
Translation is arrested/repressed (miRNA-type outcome)

//
Single-stranded RNA of ~22 nt, generated from endogenous hairpin-shaped transcripts, acting as a guide molecule in post-transcriptional gene regulation

Usually in the 3′ UTR

Roles of miRNA

Translation inhibition; mRNA cleavage (if perfectly complementary); heterochromatin formation

First miRNA discovered

Four genomic-location classes of miRNA genes

(a) intronic in a non-coding transcript, (b) exonic in a non-coding transcript, (c) intronic in a protein-coding transcript, (d) exonic in a protein-coding transcript

Intragenic miRNA (~40%)

(1) Nuclear processing by Drosha, (2) nuclear export by Exportin-5, (3) cytoplasmic processing by Dicer

pri-miRNA
//
The long, 7-methylguanosine-capped and polyadenylated primary transcript made by RNA Pol II transcribing a miRNA gene

Microprocessor complex

Drosha (RNase III) + DGCR8/Pasha; cleaves pri-miRNA ~11 nt from the ds–ss junction to give pre-miRNA

~60–80 nt hairpin with a 2-nt 3′ overhang and 5′-P/3′-OH ends

Nuclear export of pre-miRNA (animals)

Strand selection rule for miRISC loading
//
The guide strand is retained in miRISC (Argonaute); the passenger strand is degraded. Selection favors the strand whose 5′ end is thermodynamically less stable (also 5′-U at position 1, weak base pairing)

#flashcards/bmb501/viroids

Viroid (definition)
//
The smallest known infectious agent — much simpler than a virus

Viroid structure
//
Only a short strand of circular, single-stranded RNA; no protein coat (capsid); no envelope

Viroid hosts
//
Infect plants only (not animals or bacteria)

Viroid function
//
Do not code for proteins; interfere with the plant's cellular processes, disrupting gene expression

Diseases caused by viroids
//
Potato spindle tuber disease, citrus exocortis

Viroid replication mechanism
//
Rolling-circle replication: (+) strand → synthesis of (−) strand → self-cleavage by the viroid ribozyme → circularization, and the reverse process for the (+) strand

How does a viroid interfere with host gene expression?
//
The viroid RNA folds into a specific shape; the plant cell treats it as abnormal RNA and dices it into small interfering RNAs (siRNA), which then bind normal plant mRNA and block or degrade it

#flashcards/bmb501/sirna

siRNA (short interfering/silencing RNA) structure
//
19–21 bp core duplex with a 2-nt 3′ overhang at both ends

Sources of siRNA processed by Dicer
//
Long dsRNA (gene duplication/pseudogene), hairpin RNA (shRNA), or transfection (shRNA/expression vector under a U6/H1 promoter)

Why is shRNA better than direct siRNA transfection?
//
Dicer-processed siRNA is protected from RNase H by incorporation into RISC — direct transfected siRNA lacks this protection

Endo-siRNA triggers — six sources of dsRNA precursors
//
Structured loci pairing intramolecularly; convergent transcription/complementary overlapping transcripts; read-through transcription of transposons in inverted orientation; bidirectional transcription; trans-interaction (founder gene pairing with a pseudogene); duplicated and inverted pseudogene copies

Pseudogene origin — unitary pseudogene
//
Arises by gene duplication + divergence

Pseudogene origin — processed pseudogene
//
Arises by reverse transcription + integration of mRNA; lacks introns and upstream promoters — considered "dead on arrival"

Pseudogene Route 1 (dsRNA → siRNA)
//
Sense (parent) + antisense (pseudogene) transcripts form a duplex → Dicer → 21 bp siRNA → RISC → mRNA silencing

Pseudogene Route 2 (shRNA)
//
A duplicated-and-inverted copy is transcribed, folds into shRNA, diced by Dicer → siRNA → RISC loading → mRNA silencing

Pseudogene Route 3 (ceRNA)
//
Pseudogene transcripts act as competing endogenous RNAs, sharing miRNA target sites in their 3′-UTR; by sponging miRNAs away from the parental mRNA, competition up-regulates parental gene expression

ceRNA stands for
//
Competing endogenous RNA

siRNA vs miRNA — precursor/processing
//
siRNA: long dsRNA (exo- or endogenous), needs Dicer. miRNA: stem-loop (hairpin) precursor from genomic loci, needs Dicer

siRNA vs miRNA — origin
//
siRNA: transgene, virus, active transposon (exo/endo). miRNA: endogenous cellular RNA

siRNA vs miRNA — homology to target
//
siRNA: high/perfect complementarity. miRNA: can tolerate mismatches (seed match)

siRNA vs miRNA — target site
//
siRNA: anywhere on the mRNA. miRNA: usually the 3′-UTR

siRNA vs miRNA — target effect
//
siRNA: cleavage of target mRNA by RISC. miRNA: translation block, or cleavage if perfect

siRNA vs miRNA — biological role
//
siRNA: defence vs transgenes, viruses, transposons. miRNA: development, cell proliferation and death

siRNA vs miRNA — shared features
//
~22 nt double-stranded, 2-nt 3′ overhang; need Dicer; Argonaute-mediated silencing (both)

#flashcards/bmb501/pirna

piRNA (Piwi-interacting RNA) size
//
~26–31 nt — the largest class of small non-coding RNA in animals

piRNA abundance vs miRNA
//
Hundreds of thousands of piRNAs, vs ~1000 miRNAs in humans

Where are piRNAs mainly found?
//
Germline (mammalian testes/ovaries; invertebrate germlines); also some somatic cells

piRNA protein partner
//
Forms RNP complexes with the Piwi subfamily of Argonaute proteins

piRNA genomic origin
//
Arise from clustered transcription units (not evenly distributed); one cluster (1–100 kb) can yield 10–1000 distinct piRNAs

piRNA strand character
//
Antisense in nature; present in both cytoplasm and nucleus of germ cells

piRNA modification
//
5′ Uridine, and the 2′/3′-OH of the ribose is O-methylated (by a HEN1-like methyltransferase), enhancing stability

Key distinction of piRNA from miRNA/siRNA
//
piRNAs do NOT require RNase III (Dicer) for processing

piRNA role — transposon silencing
//
Protect the genome from 'genomic parasites' by transcriptional + post-transcriptional gene silencing

piRNA role — germ-line development
//
Early: fate specification; late: gametogenesis, egg activation and fertilization

Primary piRNA processing step 1
//
A long single-stranded precursor is transcribed from a piRNA cluster locus (often full of dead transposon sequences)

Primary piRNA processing step 2
//
The transcript is exported and processed in cytoplasmic Yb bodies (membraneless organelles/RNP condensates formed by liquid–liquid phase separation)

Primary piRNA processing step 3
//
The endonuclease Zucchini (Zuc) cleaves the transcript into intermediate RNAs

Primary piRNA processing step 4
//
Fragments with a 5′-uridine are selected and bound by Piwi

Primary piRNA processing step 5
//
An unidentified exonuclease trims the 3′ end, giving the mature piRNA

Primary piRNA processing steps 6–7
//
The Piwi/piRNA complex enters the nucleus and directs H3K9 methylation and transcriptional silencing of transposons; Piwi recruits HP1 (Heterochromatin Protein 1), which recruits the H3K9 methyltransferase

Secondary piRNA processing — where it operates
//
Germ cells (the ping-pong amplification loop)

Ping-pong loop step 1
//
Aub (bound to an antisense primary piRNA) cleaves an active transposon mRNA, forming the 5′ end of a new sense piRNA that is bound by Ago3

Ping-pong loop step 2
//
The Ago3/sense-piRNA complex cleaves piRNA-cluster transcripts to make new antisense piRNAs bound by Aub, reinforcing the antisense pool

Ping-pong signature
//
A characteristic 10-nt 5′ overlap between sense (Ago3) and antisense (Aub) piRNAs; HEN1 methylates and a Trimmer trims the 3′ ends

P-element transposon
//
2.9 kb, 31-bp inverted repeat; encodes P-transposase; when it mobilizes in the germline it produces infertile (dysgenic) progeny

How does a P-cytotype female prevent dysgenesis?
//
She expresses a P-inhibitor that chops down P-element RNA → no genome disruption → fertile progeny

P-element splicing in somatic cells
//
Protein binds intron 3 and prevents splicing → 66 kD repressor → no transposition

P-element splicing in germline cells
//
Intron 3 is spliced → 87 kD transposase → transposition

P-cytotype vs M-cytotype
//
P-present = P-cytotype (paternal-contributing); P-absent = M-cytotype (maternal-contributing)

Source of piRNA in hybrid dysgenesis
//
Produced from the mother's X-chromosome telomere, carrying the P-element sequence; deposited maternally via nurse cells/cytoplasmic bridges

Cross M(♂) × M(♀) — result and why
//
Normal; no P-element present at all

Cross M(♂) × P(♀) — result and why
//
Normal, no rearrangement; P-female supplies piRNA (+66 kD) to the oocyte, degrading P-element RNA — no P-element movement

Cross P(♂) × M(♀) — result and why
//
Dysgenic; M-female has no piRNA/66 kD; paternal P-elements make transposase and move; piRNA cannot be transmitted through sperm

Cross P(♂) × P(♀) — result and why
//
Normal; P-female supplies piRNA (+66 kD), degrading P-element RNA from both strains; no movement
