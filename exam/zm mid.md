## SECTION 1: TUMOR IMMUNOLOGY

### 1.1 Terminology (Tumor Biology)

- **Neoplasm:** Cells that give rise to clones of cells that can expand in an uncontrolled manner will produce a tumor or neoplasm.
- **Malignant:** A tumor that continues to grow and becomes progressively more invasive.
- **Benign:** A tumor that is not capable of indefinite growth and does not invade the healthy surrounding tissue extensively.
- **Metastasis:** The process in which small clusters of cancerous cells dislodge from the original tumor, invade the blood or lymphatic vessels, and are carried to other distant tissues, where they take up residence and continue to proliferate.

**Classification of Malignant Tumors:**
- **Carcinomas:** Tumors that arise from epithelial origins (skin, gut, epithelial lining of internal organs and glands). Examples: skin cancers, colon, breast, prostate, lung.
- **Leukemias, Lymphomas, and Myelomas:** Malignant tumors of hematopoietic cells derived from bone marrow.
- **Sarcoma:** Derived from mesodermal connective tissues (bone, fat, cartilage).

### 1.2 Tumor Antigens

**Definition:** An antigenic substance produced in tumor cells that triggers an immune response in the host. Proteins expressed on the surface of tumor cells or secreted into the bloodstream.

**Classification by T cells (4 groups based on source):**
1. Antigens encoded by genes exclusively expressed by tumors (e.g., viral genes)
2. Antigens encoded by variant forms of normal genes altered by mutation
3. Antigens normally expressed only at certain stages of development
4. Antigens that are overexpressed in particular tumors

**Two major classes by uniqueness:**
1. **Tumor-Specific Antigens (TSA):** Found only on cancer cells, not on healthy cells
2. **Tumor-Associated Antigens (TAA):** Highly expressed on tumor cells, but also expressed at lower levels on healthy cells

**TSAs:**
- Unique to tumor cells
- Do NOT occur on normal cells
- Result from mutation in tumor cells generating altered cellular proteins
- Found on tumors that are chemically/physically/virally induced
- Presented with class I MHC molecules
- Induce cell-mediated response by tumor-specific CTL

**TAAs:**
- NOT unique to tumor cells
- DO occur on normal cells
- Typically expressed only during specific developmental stages (fetal) or at extremely low levels
- Upregulated on tumor cells

**Mechanism of Generating TSAs & TAAs:**
- Mutation generates new peptide in class I MHC molecule (TSTA)
- Inappropriate expression of embryonic gene (TATA)
- Overexpression of normal protein (TATA)

**Modern Classification of Tumor Antigens:**
1. Products of Mutated Oncogenes and Tumor Suppressor Genes
2. Products of Other Mutated Genes
3. Overexpressed or Aberrantly Expressed Cellular Proteins
4. Tumor Antigens Produced by Oncogenic Viruses
5. Oncofetal Antigens
6. Altered Cell Surface Glycolipids and Glycoproteins
7. Cell Type-Specific Differentiation Antigens

**Normal Cellular Genes Associated with Cancer:**
1. Oncogenes
2. Tumor-suppressor genes (anti-oncogenes)
3. Genes involved in programmed cell death (apoptosis)
   - Pro-apoptotic genes (act like tumor suppressors)
   - Anti-apoptotic genes (act like oncogenes)

**Examples of Overexpressed Cellular Proteins:**
- **EGF receptor:** Expressed on tumor cells at levels 100 times greater than normal
- **Melanotransferrin (p97):** Normal cells <8,000 molecules/cell; Melanoma cells 50,000–500,000 molecules/cell
- **Cancer-testis antigens:** MAGE (melanoma-associated antigens)
- **HER2/Neu:** Overexpressed in some breast cancers
- **Differentiation antigens:** Tyrosinase, MART-1 (Melan-A) in melanomas

**Neoantigens:**
- Unique, newly formed proteins/peptides on tumor cell surface due to genetic mutations
- Not present in healthy cells
- Recognized as "non-self" → immune response
- Products of randomly mutated genes ("passenger mutations") or mutated oncogenes/tumor suppressor genes ("driver mutations")

**Viral Tumor Antigens:**
- Products of oncogenic viruses function as tumor antigens
- Elicit specific T cell responses
- Examples: EBV (B cell lymphomas, nasopharyngeal carcinoma), HPV (cervical, oropharyngeal carcinomas)
- Virus-encoded protein antigens found in nucleus, cytoplasm, or plasma membrane
- Processed and presented by MHC molecules

**Oncofetal Antigens:**
- Proteins expressed at high levels in cancer cells and fetal but not adult tissues
- Expression in adults not limited to tumors; increased in inflammatory conditions
- Found in small quantities in normal adult tissues
- No evidence as important inducers of antitumor immunity
- Limited usefulness as tumor markers, targets of antibodies, or vaccine candidates
- Two most studied: CEA (carcinoembryonic antigen) and AFP (alpha-fetoprotein)

**AFP:**
- Abundant fetal protein
- Drops from milligram levels in fetal serum to 5–50 ng/ml after birth
- Elevated in women during early pregnancy
- Significantly elevated in nonpregnant adults in ovarian, testicular, and liver cancers
- Above 300 ng/ml indicative of small lesions even in asymptomatic individuals
- Monitoring helps prognoses and evaluate treatment efficacy (especially liver cancer)

**CEA (Oncofetal membrane glycoprotein):**
- Found on GI tract and liver cells of 2–6 month old fetuses
- Increased in serum of ~90% patients with advanced colorectal cancer
- ~50% patients with early colorectal cancer
- Also found in trace amounts in some normal adults and non-cancerous cases
- Useful as diagnostic marker to monitor tumor growth

**Altered Glycolipid and Glycoprotein Antigens:**
- Most human and experimental tumors express higher than normal levels or abnormal forms of surface glycoproteins and glycolipids
- Includes gangliosides, blood group antigens, and mucins
- Example: MUC-1 gene in breast and other cancers

### 1.3 Immune Surveillance

**Definition:** A physiologic function of the immune system to recognize and destroy clones of transformed cells before they grow into tumors and to kill tumors after they are formed. Proposed by Macfarlane Burnet in the 1950s.

**Three proposed mechanisms:**
1. By destroying viruses that are known to transform cells
2. By eliminating pathogens and reducing pro-tumor inflammation
3. By actively identifying and eliminating cancerous cells (Immunosurveillance)

**Evidence:**
- AIDS patients and transplant recipients on immunosuppressive drugs have much higher incidence of several types of cancer
- Immunocompromised experimental animals show increased incidence of some types of tumors
- Many tumors are surrounded by mononuclear cell infiltrates (T lymphocytes and macrophages)
- Activated lymphocytes and macrophages present in lymph nodes draining sites of tumor growth

**Relative Risk of Tumors in Immunosuppressed Kidney Transplant Patients:**
| Tumor Type | Approximate Relative Risk |
|---|---|
| Kaposi's sarcoma | 50–100 |
| Non-Hodgkin's lymphoma | 25–45 |
| Carcinoma of the liver | 20–35 |
| Carcinoma of the skin | 20–50 |
| Carcinoma of the cervix | 2.5–10 |
| Melanoma | 2.5–10 |
| Lung | 1–2 |

**Immunogenicity of Chemically Induced Tumors:**
- Mice immunized with tumor cells are protected against subsequent tumor growth
- The primary animal that develops the tumor is also immune to subsequent challenges with the same tumor

### 1.4 Immune Responses to Tumors

**Key Features:**
1. Tumors stimulate specific adaptive immune responses that can prevent or limit growth and spread
   - Most evidence: T-cells, especially CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs)
   - Histopathologic studies: Tumors surrounded by mononuclear cell infiltrates (T lymphocytes and macrophages)
   - Activated lymphocytes and macrophages present in lymph nodes draining tumor sites
   - Higher density of CD3+ T cells reflects stronger anti-tumor immune response
   - Lymphocyte infiltration associated with better survival (Kaplan-Meier plot)

2. Immune responses frequently fail to prevent tumor growth
   - Many tumors develop specialized mechanisms for subverting host immune responses
   - Tumor cells lose expression of antigens recognized by host immune system

3. Ineffective adaptive immune responses can be overcome by therapeutic strategies
   - Anti-tumor T cells can be activated to effectively kill tumor cells

**Innate Immune Responses:**
- **NK Cells:** Recognize and kill tumor cells directly; induce apoptosis without prior sensitization
- **Macrophages:** Phagocytose tumor cells; present tumor antigens to activate T cells; TAMs have both pro-tumoral and anti-tumoral functions
- **Neutrophils:** Release cytotoxic molecules to damage tumor cells

**Antitumourigenic vs Protumourigenic TME:**
| Antitumourigenic TME | Protumourigenic TME |
|---|---|
| M1 macrophages | M2 macrophages/MDSCs |
| Th1 cells | Th2 cells |
| Mature dendritic cells | Tolerogenic dendritic cells |
| CD8+ cytotoxic T cells | Regulatory T cells (Tregs) |
| NK cells | Immunosuppressive myeloid cells |
| IFN-γ, IL-12, TNF-α | IL-10, TGF-β, VEGF |
| Strong immune activation | Immune suppression |
| Tumor cell killing | Tumor growth |
| Reduced angiogenesis | Increased angiogenesis |
| Better prognosis | Poor prognosis and metastasis |

**M1 Macrophages (Anti-tumorigenic):**
- Activated by microbial products (LPS) and pro-inflammatory cytokines (IFN-γ)
- Cytokines produced: IL-12, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, ROS, NO
- Functions:
  1. Direct killing of tumor cells (ROS, NO, lysosomal enzymes, TNF-α)
  2. Phagocytosis
  3. Antigen presentation via MHC class II
  4. Activation of cytotoxic immune cells (CD8+ CTLs, NK cells, Th1 cells)
  5. Inhibition of angiogenesis

**M2 Macrophages (Pro-tumorigenic):**
- Induced by IL-4, IL-10, IL-13, TGF-β
- Cytokines produced: IL-10, TGF-β, VEGF, EGF, MMP-2, MMP-9, Arginase-1
- Functions:
  1. Suppression of anti-tumor immunity (inhibit CD8+ T cells, NK cells, DCs, Th1 cells)
  2. Promotion of angiogenesis (VEGF, PDGF, FGF)
  3. Tissue remodeling and invasion (MMPs)
  4. Promotion of metastasis
  5. Tumor growth (EGF)
  6. Induction of regulatory T cells

**Neutrophil Anti-tumor Mechanisms:**
A. Neutrophil-mediated immune activation:
- NET constituents induce priming of CD4+ T cells
- APC-like neutrophils enhance T cell and NK cell-mediated antitumor responses
- IL-12 from macrophages stimulates neutrophils to release IFN-γ
- ADCC during antibody treatment

B. Neutrophil-mediated killing of tumor cells:
- TNF-α increases neutrophil recruitment by increasing MET expression
- HGF activates MET+ neutrophils → destroy cancer cells by releasing NO
- Neutrophils at premetastatic locations use H2O2 to destroy invading cancer cells
- TRAIL and FasL induce apoptosis
- MMP secretion causes tumor cell death

**Neutrophil Pro-tumor Mechanisms:**
A. Cancer initiation:
- ROS and proteases (elastase) interfere with DNA repair
- Accelerate DNA damage and increase mutation load

B. Cancer cell proliferation:
- Neutrophil elastase (NE) degrades IRS1 → activates PI3K/AKT signaling
- MMP9, HMGB1, G-CSF, IL-1, IL-6 trigger tumor cell proliferation

C. ECM remodeling:
- MMP-8, MMP-9, NE, Cathepsin G disintegrate ECM and basement membranes

**T-Cell Response Against Tumor:**
- Principal mechanism: Killing of tumor cells by CD8+ CTLs
- CTLs perform surveillance function by recognizing and killing potentially malignant cells expressing tumor antigens presented by class I MHC
- Tumor-specific CTLs can be isolated from animals and humans with established tumors
- Prognosis more favorable when more CTLs are present within tumor (e.g., colonic carcinomas)

**Cross-Presentation:**
- Tumor antigens picked up by host dendritic cells
- Processed inside APCs
- Peptides displayed bound to class I MHC molecules
- Recognized by CD8+ T cells
- This process is called cross-presentation or cross-priming

**Why Cross-Presentation is Needed:**
- Most tumor antigens not derived from APCs
- Most tumor cells do not express costimulators (B7-1/CD80, B7-2/CD86)
- Most tumor cells do not express class II MHC molecules

**CD8+ T Cell Response:**
- Tumor antigens picked up by host dendritic cells
- Responses initiated in peripheral (secondary) lymphoid organs
- Tumor-specific CTLs migrate back to tumor and kill tumor cells

**CD4+ T Cell Response:**
- Activation of CTLs by releasing cytokines (IL-2, IFN-γ)
- Promotion of dendritic cell activation
- Enhancement of memory T-cell formation
- Facilitation of B-cell responses
- Regulation of immune responses
- Induction of inflammatory responses
- Modulation of immune checkpoints
- Tumor microenvironment regulation

**CD4+ T Cell Differentiation:**
| Cytokine Environment | CD4+ Subset | Major Cytokines | Major Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| IL-12 | Th1 | IFN-γ | Protection against intracellular pathogens; macrophage activation |
| IL-4 | Th2 | IL-4, IL-5, IL-13 | Protection against helminths; activation of eosinophils; allergy; IgE |
| TGF-β + IL-6/IL-1 | Th17 | IL-17, IL-22 | Extracellular bacteria/fungi; neutrophil recruitment |
| TGF-β | Treg | IL-10, TGF-β, FOXP3 | Immune suppression and tolerance |

**NK Cell Response:**
- Most potent cytotoxic cells against tumor cells
- NK cell deficient mice show increased incidence of lymphomas and sarcomas
- Impairment of NK cells associated with increase of certain types of cancer
- Tumor cells become susceptible when they down-regulate class I MHC or upregulate activating ligands
- Loss of class I MHC makes tumors good targets for NK cells

**Activation of NK Cell Killing:**
- Normal cells express MHC I → inhibit activation of NK cells
- Stressed cells downregulate MHC-I → inhibitory signaling lost → activation of NK cells ("missing-self recognition")
- NK cell activating receptors recognize:
  - Pathogen-encoded molecules ("non-self recognition")
  - Self-expressed proteins altered or upregulated by transformed cells ("stress-induced self recognition")
  - Stress ligands: MICA, MICB, ULBPs → recognized by NKG2D

**Direct Tumor Clearance by NK:**
- Target human tumor cells that:
  1. Have lost self MHC-I expression
  2. Bear "altered-self" stress-inducible proteins
- Mechanisms:
  a) Release cytoplasmic granules (perforin, granzymes) → apoptosis
  b) Death receptor-mediated apoptosis
  c) Secrete effector molecules (IFN-γ, NO)
  d) ADCC

**NK Cell Killing Mechanisms:**
a) ADCC: Fc portion of antibody binds Fc receptor on NK cell
b) Fas (CD95)-Fas ligand (CD95L) interaction
c) Release of perforin and granzyme B → apoptosis/necrosis

**Death Receptor-Mediated Apoptosis:**
- NK cells express FasL or TRAIL
- Induce apoptosis by interacting with Fas and TRAIL receptor on tumor cells
- TNF-α produced by NK cells can induce apoptosis

**Cytokine Enhancement of NK Activity:**
| Cytokine | Main Function | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| IL-2 | Promotes NK-cell proliferation and activation | ↑ Perforin/granzymes → ↑ tumor-cell killing |
| IL-12 | Activates NK cells, stimulates IFN-γ production | ↑ Cytotoxicity and macrophage activation |
| IL-18 | Stimulates NK cells to produce IFN-γ | ↑ NK-cell cytotoxicity and anti-tumor response |
| IL-15 | Promotes NK-cell development, survival, proliferation | Maintains and enhances NK-cell population |

### 1.5 Role of Immunity in Promoting Tumor Growth

**General:**
- Immune system designed to recognize and eliminate abnormal/cancerous cells
- Tumors develop strategies to evade immune surveillance
- Chronic inflammation is a risk factor for tumors (Barrett's esophagus, ulcerative colitis)
- Cancers associated with infections: gastric carcinoma and lymphoma (H. pylori), hepatocellular carcinomas (hepatitis B and C)

**Innate Immunity in Promoting Tumor Growth:**
- M2 macrophages and MDSCs are sources of VEGF (promotes angiogenesis) and MMPs (modify extracellular tissue)
- Chronic activation → angiogenesis and tissue remodeling → favor tumor growth and spread
- Free radicals cause DNA damage → mutations in tumor suppressor genes and oncogenes
- Mast cells, neutrophils, macrophages secrete soluble factors (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-10, chemokines, growth factors) → promote cell cycle progression and survival
- NF-κB plays important role in inflammation-associated cancer progression
- M2 macrophages and MDSCs inhibit effective antitumor immunity

**Adaptive Immunity in Promoting Tumor Growth:**
- Dendritic cells conditioned to drive CD4+ T differentiation to anti-inflammatory Th2 cells or Tregs
- Both suppress immune responses that destroy tumors
- Increase development of M2 macrophages/N2 neutrophils
- B lymphocytes secrete factors that directly regulate proliferation of tumor cells
- Chronically activate innate immune cells present in early tumors

**Tumor Microenvironment in Promoting Tumor Growth (Figure 18.5):**
- Tumors alter phenotype of DCs → promote differentiation of anti-inflammatory Treg and Th2 cells
- Treg and Th2 cells promote differentiation and accumulation of M2 macrophages and MDSCs
- M2 macrophages and MDSCs block action of antitumor CTLs and Th1 cells
- Provide growth factors for tumor cells and tumor blood vessels

---

### 1.6 Evasion of Immune Responses by Tumor

**General:**
- Ability to evade host immunity is a biologic hallmark of tumors
- Many tumors are successful at immune evasion
- Several mechanisms hypothesized and supported by experimental evidence

**Mechanisms:**
1. **Downregulation of Antigen Presentation:**
   - Loss of MHC-I molecules
   - Impaired ability to present antigens to cytotoxic T cells

2. **Creation of Immunosuppressive Microenvironment:**
   - Recruitment of immunosuppressive cells (Tregs, MDSCs)
   - TAMs adopt pro-tumorigenic phenotype

3. **Inhibition of T Cell Function:**
   - Expression of immune checkpoints (PD-L1)
   - Release of immunosuppressive molecules (TGF-β, IL-10)

4. **Resistance to Cell Death:**
   - Apoptosis resistance
   - Upregulation of anti-apoptotic proteins (Bcl-2)

5. **Antigen Heterogeneity and Loss:**
   - Antigenic variation
   - Loss of tumor antigens

6. **Production of Immunosuppressive Proteins:**
   - Inhibitory ligands (PD-L1)
   - Inhibitory receptors (PD-1)
   - Immunosuppressive cytokines (TGF-β)

7. **Failure to Produce Tumor Antigen:**
   - Antigen-loss variant of tumor cell

8. **Mutations in MHC Genes:**
   - Class I MHC-deficient tumor cell
   - Mutations in genes needed for antigen processing (TAP1/TAP2, Tapasin, HLA-DM)

### 1.7 Immunotherapy for Tumors

**History of Cancer Immunotherapy:**
- 1863: Description of immune infiltrates by Virchow
- 1898: Treatment of cancer with bacterial products ("Coley's toxin")
- 1957: Cancer immunosurveillance hypothesis
- 1976: Treatment of bladder cancer with BCG
- 1983: IL-2 therapy for cancer
- 1985: Adoptive cell therapy
- 1991: Discovery of human tumor antigens
- 2002: Adoptive T cell therapy
- 2009: HPV vaccination to prevent uterine cervical neoplasia
- 2010: FDA approval of DC vaccine (sipuleucel-T) for prostate cancer
- 2011: FDA approval of anti-CTLA4 (ipilimumab) for melanoma
- 2014: Breakthrough status for CAR-T cells in leukemia
- 2016: FDA approval of anti-PD-1, PD-L1 for many cancers

**Checkpoint Blockade:**
- Cell-cell signaling necessary for T cell activation and inactivation
- Signals necessary to activate and shut down a T cell
- Balance of signals regulates strength and duration of immune response

**CTLA-4 Immune Checkpoint Pathway:**
- When CTLA-4 connects instead of other molecules, signal to shut down (-) is sent to T cell
- Anti-CTLA4 blocks CTLA-4 → unleashes proliferation & clonal expansion

**PD-1/PD-L1 Immune Checkpoint Pathway:**
- When PD-1 connects to PD-L1, signal to shut down (-) is sent to T cell → T cell becomes inactive
- Anti-PD-1/PD-L1 blocks interaction → T cell reactivation

**Checkpoint Inhibitors:**
- CTLA-4 inhibitors: Ipilimumab, Tremelimumab
- PD-1 inhibitors: Pembrolizumab, Nivolumab
- PD-L1 inhibitors: Atezolizumab, Avelumab, Durvalumab

**Vaccination With Tumor Antigens:**
- Vaccination of tumor-bearing individuals with tumor antigens may result in enhanced immune responses
- Features providing candidate antigens:
  i) Identification of peptides recognized by tumor-specific CTLs
  ii) Cloning of genes encoding tumor-specific antigens recognized by CTLs
- New DNA sequencing technologies determine all mutations in protein coding DNA sequences (exomes)
- MHC-binding prediction algorithms identify mutant peptides
- Technical advances allow precise identification of tumor-specific neoantigens → personalized vaccination approaches
- Adjuvants: TLR ligands (CpG DNA, dsRNA mimics), cytokines (GM-CSF, IL-12)
- Tumor antigens delivered as dendritic cell vaccines

**Dendritic Cell Vaccines:**
- Dendritic cells purified from patients
- Incubated with tumor antigens
- Injected back into patients
- Cell-based vaccine approved for advanced prostate cancer
- Composed of patient's peripheral blood leukocytes enriched for dendritic cells
- Exposed to recombinant fusion protein (GM-CSF + PAP)
- GM-CSF promotes maturation of dendritic cells
- Technical challenges: Cells harvested from each patient, require expansion in cell culture, difficult to standardize

**Adoptive Cellular Therapy With Antitumor T Cells:**
- Transfer of cultured immune cells with antitumor reactivity into tumor-bearing host
- Immune cells derived from cancer patient's blood or solid tumor
- Treated in vitro to expand numbers and enhance antitumor activity
- Reinfused back into patient

**Key Components and Steps:**
1. Collection of immune cells (T cells from patient's own body or donor)
2. Ex vivo modification (genetic engineering with CARs)
3. Expansion and activation in laboratory
4. Infusion back into patient's bloodstream
5. Targeting cancer cells
6. Persistence and memory
7. Monitoring and follow-up

**Two common types:**
- CAR T-cell Therapy
- Tumor-Infiltrating Lymphocyte (TIL) Therapy

**CAR T-Cell Therapy:**
- T cells isolated from blood expanded by culture in IL-2, anti-CD3, anti-CD28
- Genetically modified to express recombinant CARs
- Transferred back into patient
- CARs composed of:
  - Extracellular Ig single chain variable fragment (scFv) specific for tumor antigen
  - Cytoplasmic signaling domains: TCR complex ζ chain ITAMs, motifs in cytoplasmic domain of costimulatory receptors (CD28, 4-1BB)
- Reason for using Ig binding site: Avoids MHC restriction of TCRs → same CAR construct can be used in any patient
- Ig binding site attached to genetically engineered cytoplasmic tail containing signaling domains

**A typical CAR has four major components:**
| Component | Structure/Example | Function |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Antigen-binding domain | scFv derived from antibody | Recognizes and binds specific tumor-associated antigen independently of MHC |
| 2. Hinge/Spacer region | Derived from IgG or CD8α | Provides flexibility and appropriate distance |
| 3. Transmembrane domain | CD3ζ, CD8α, or CD28-derived region | Anchors CAR in T-cell membrane; contributes to receptor stability |
| 4. Intracellular signaling domain | CD3ζ plus co-stimulatory domains (CD28, 4-1BB) | Activates T cell after antigen binding; promotes cytokine production, proliferation, cytotoxicity |

**CAR Generations:**
- First generation: ITAM only (Signal 1)
- Second generation: One co-stimulatory molecule (CM1) + ITAM (Signal 1 + Signal 2)
- Third generation: Second CM + ITAM (Signal 1 + Signal 2 + Signal 2)
- Fourth generation (TRUCKs): Second-gen CAR + constitutively/inducibly expressed chemokine (IL-12)
- Fifth generation: Second-gen CAR + intracellular domains of cytokine receptors (IL-2Rβ chain fragment)

**TIL Therapy:**
- T cells specific for tumor antigens harvested from patient's tumor tissue or blood
- Expanded and activated in vitro
- Reinfused into cancer patients
- Steps:
  a. Surgical resection
  b. Fragmentation
  c. Initial expansion (Young TIL reserves, tumor cell line)
  d. Rapid Expansion Protocol (REP): Irr feeder cells, OKT3, IL-2
  e. Ex vivo analyses & QC
  f. Infusion product
  g. Treatment: IL-2, Chemo-lymphodepletion

**Passive Immunotherapy With Antibodies:**
- Transfer of tumor-specific antibodies into patients
- Rapid and theoretically very specific
- Does not lead to long-lived immunity
- Some antitumor antibodies bind cell surface molecules on tumor cells → engage host effector mechanisms
- Other monoclonal antibodies bind growth factor receptors → interfere with signaling required for tumor growth and survival

**Bispecific T Cell Engagers (BiTEs):**
- Facilitate targeting of host T cells of any specificity to attack tumor cells
- Recombinant antibodies engineered to express two different antigen binding sites
- One specific for tumor antigen
- Second specific for T cell surface molecule (usually CD3)

**Immunotoxins (Conjugated Monoclonal Antibodies):**
- Antibodies specific for tumor antigens linked to chemotherapy drug or radioisotope

### 1.8 Apoptosis Mechanisms

**Extrinsic Pathway:**
- FasL binds Fas → FADD → Procaspase-8 → DISC → Caspase-8 → Caspase-3 → ICAD cleavage → CAD release → DNA fragmentation
- TNF binds TNFR1 → TRADD and FADD → Procaspase-8 → multiprotein complex → Caspase-8 → Executioner caspases → Apoptosis

**Intrinsic Pathway:**
- Cellular stress → BH3-only proteins → inhibit BCL-2 → BAK/BAX activation → MOMP → Cytochrome c release → Apaf-1 + Procaspase-9 → Apoptosome → Caspase-9 → Caspase-3/7 → Cell death
- Smac/Diablo released → sequesters IAPs → permits cell death
- ICAD cleavage by caspase-3 → CAD → nucleus → DNA cleavage

**Perforin/Granzyme Pathway:**
- Granzyme B synthesized in CTLs and NK cells
- Transported into target cell through pore made by perforin
- Inside target cell, Gr-B recruits caspase 3 and 7 → apoptosis
- Gr-B can also activate apoptosis through mitochondrial-mediated pathway

## SECTION 2: IMMUNODEFICIENCY

### 2.1 General Features

**Definition:** A condition characterized by a weakened or compromised immune system.

**Classification:**
1. **Primary (congenital) immunodeficiency:** Genetic defects resulting in increased susceptibility to infection; manifested in infancy/early childhood but sometimes clinically detected later
2. **Secondary (acquired) immunodeficiency:** Not inherited; develop as consequence of malnutrition, disseminated cancer, treatment with immunosuppressive drugs, or infection of immune cells (most notably HIV)

**General Features of Immunodeficiency:**
1. Principal consequence: Increased susceptibility to infection
2. Susceptible to certain types of cancer (oncogenic viruses: EBV, HPVs)
3. Increased incidence of autoimmunity (incomplete loss of immune population/function → attenuation of regulatory mechanism)
4. May result from defects in lymphocyte development or activation, or defects in effector mechanisms

**Features of Immunodeficiencies Affecting T or B Lymphocytes:**
| Feature | B Cell Deficiency | T Cell Deficiency |
|---|---|---|
| Susceptibility to infection | Pyogenic bacteria (otitis, pneumonia, meningitis, osteomyelitis), enteric bacteria and viruses, some parasites | Pneumocystis jiroveci, many viruses, atypical mycobacteria, fungi |
| Serum Ig levels | Reduced | Normal or reduced |
| DTH reactions to common antigens | Normal | Reduced |
| Morphology of lymphoid tissues | Absent or reduced follicles and germinal centers (B cell zones) | Usually normal follicles, may be reduced parafollicular cortical regions (T cell zones) |

### 2.2 Primary (Congenital) Immunodeficiencies

#### A. Defects in Innate Immunity

**1. Chronic Granulomatous Disease (CGD)**
- Rare, inherited disorder
- Affects phagocytes → prevents effective killing of bacteria and fungi
- Recurrent and potentially severe infections
- Formation of granulomas (masses of inflammatory cells)
- ~2/3 cases: X-linked recessive (mutation in CYBB gene encoding gp91phox)
- Remainder: Autosomal recessive (mutations in CYBA, NCF1, NCF2)
- Defective production of superoxide anion → failure to kill phagocytosed microbes
- Catalase-positive pathogens particularly troublesome (Staphylococcus, Aspergillus)
- Invasive Aspergillus infection — leading cause of death
- Infections stimulate chronic cell-mediated immune responses → T cell-mediated macrophage activation → granulomas
- IFN-γ enhances transcription of phox91 gene → stimulates superoxide production
- IFN-γ therapy commonly used for X-linked CGD

**2. Leukocyte Adhesion Deficiency Type 1 (LAD-I)**
- Defective leukocyte adhesion to endothelial cells and migration into tissues
- Decreased or absent expression of β2 integrins
- Recurrent bacterial and fungal infections
- Mutations in ITGB2 gene encoding CD18 (β chain of β2 integrins)

**3. Leukocyte Adhesion Deficiency Type 2 (LAD-II)**
- Defective leukocyte rolling and migration into tissues
- Decreased or absent expression of leukocyte ligands for endothelial E- and P-selectins
- Failure of leukocyte migration into tissues
- Recurrent bacterial and fungal infections
- Mutations in SLC35C1 gene encoding GDP-fucose transporter-1

**4. Leukocyte Adhesion Deficiency Type 3 (LAD-III)**
- Defective leukocyte adhesion and migration into tissues
- Defective chemokine-stimulated integrin activation
- Mutations in FERMT3 gene encoding KINDLIN-3 (cytoskeletal protein linked to integrin activation)

**5. Chédiak-Higashi Syndrome**
- Rare autosomal recessive disorder
- Recurrent infections by pyogenic bacteria
- Partial oculocutaneous albinism
- Infiltration of various organs by nonneoplastic lymphocytes
- Neutrophils, monocytes, lymphocytes contain giant lysosomes
- Mutations in LYST gene → defective intracellular trafficking of lysosomes
- Defective phagosome-lysosome fusion → reduced resistance to infection
- Defective melanosome formation → albinism
- Lysosomal abnormalities in nervous system → nerve defects
- Lysosomal abnormalities in platelets → bleeding disorders
- Giant lysosomes form in neutrophils during maturation

**6. NK Cell Deficiencies**
- Reduced or absent NK cells
- Mutations in GATA2 (transcription factor) or MCM4 (DNA helicase)
- GATA2: Essential for development, survival, differentiation of HSCs
- MCM4: Subunit of MCM complex; acts as replicative DNA helicase

**7. Toll-Like Receptor (TLR) Signaling Defects**
- Recurrent infections caused by defects in TLR and CD40 signaling
- Defective type I interferon production
- Mutations in TLR3, TRIF, TBK1, NEMO, UNC93B, MyD88, IκBα, IRAK-4
- Compromise NF-κB activation downstream of TLR

**8. Mendelian Susceptibility to Mycobacterial Diseases (MSMD)**
- Severe disease caused by nontuberculous environmental mycobacteria, BCG, and other intracellular bacteria
- Mutations in IL-12p40, IL-12Rβ, IFNGR1, IFNGR2, STAT1, NEMO, ISG15

---

#### B. Severe Combined Immunodeficiencies (SCID)

**Definition:** Immunodeficiencies affecting both humoral and cell-mediated immunity. Result from impaired T lymphocyte development with or without defects in B cell maturation. Patients suffer from severe infections (pneumonia, meningitis, bacteremia).

**Dangerous organisms:** P. jiroveci, viruses (VZV, CMV), rotavirus, Cryptosporidium, Giardia lamblia.

**Table 21.3 Severe Combined Immunodeficiencies:**

**Defective Thymic Development:**
- Defective pre-TCR checkpoint: Mutations in CD45, CD3D, CD3E, ORAI1, STIM1
- DiGeorge syndrome: 22q11 deletion; TBX1 transcription factor mutations
- FoxN1 deficiency: Recessive mutation in FOXN1
- TCR α chain deficiency: Autosomal recessive deletion in C region of TCR α chain
- Defective T cell thymic egress and signaling: Mutations in RHOH and MST1
- Selective loss of CD4+ T cells: Mutations in LCK and UNC119
- Bare lymphocyte syndrome: Defects in CIITA, RFXANK, RFX5, RFXAP
- MHC class I deficiency: Mutations in TAP1, TAP2, TAPASIN
- Reticular dysgenesis: Mutation in AK2

**Defects in Nucleotide Salvage Pathways:**
- ADA deficiency: Mutations in ADA gene
- PNP deficiency: Mutations in PNP gene

**Defects in Cytokine Signaling:**
- X-linked SCID: Cytokine receptor common γ chain mutations
- Autosomal recessive forms: Mutations in IL2RA, IL7RA, JAK3

**Defects in V(D)J Recombination:**
- RAG1 or RAG2 deficiency
- Double-stranded break repair and checkpoint: Mutations in ARTEMIS, DNA-PKcs, CERUNUNOS, LIG4, NBS1, MRE11, ATM

#### C. Antibody Deficiencies

**Table 21.4 Antibody Deficiencies:**

**Agammaglobulinemias:**
- X-linked: BTK mutation
- Autosomal recessive: Mutations in IgM heavy chain (μ), surrogate light chains (λ5), Igα, BLNK, PI3K p85α

**Hypogammaglobulinemias/Isotype Defects:**
- Selective IgA deficiency: Mutations in TACI in some patients
- Selective IgG2 deficiency: Deletion in IgH γ2 locus in small subset
- Common variable immunodeficiency: Mutations in ICOS and TACI in some patients
- ICF syndrome: Mutations in DNMT3B

**Hyper-IgM Syndromes:**
- X-linked: Mutation in CD40L
- Autosomal recessive with cell-mediated immune defects: Mutations in CD40, NEMO
- Autosomal recessive with antibody defect only: Mutations in AID, UNG

### 2.3 Secondary (Acquired) Immunodeficiencies

**Causes:**
1. Biologic complication of another disease (malnutrition, neoplasms, infections)
2. Iatrogenic (complication of therapy)
3. Infection targeting immune cells (HIV)

**Table 21.6 Secondary (Acquired) Immunodeficiencies:**
| Cause | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| HIV infection | Depletion of CD4+ T cells |
| Protein-calorie malnutrition | Metabolic derangements inhibit lymphocyte maturation and function |
| Irradiation and chemotherapy for cancer | Decreased bone marrow lymphocyte precursors |
| Cancer metastases and leukemia involving bone marrow | Reduced site of leukocyte development |
| Immunosuppression for transplants, autoimmune diseases | Reduced lymphocyte activation, cytokine blockade, impaired leukocyte trafficking |
| Loss of spleen | Decreased phagocytosis of blood-borne bacteria |

**HIV/AIDS:**
- Most important chemokine receptors acting as coreceptors: CCR5, CXCR4
- HIV life cycle: Binding → Fusion → Entry → Reverse transcription → Integration → Transcription → Synthesis → Assembly → Budding
- Pathogenesis: Acute infection → Chronic phase → AIDS
- Mechanisms of CD4+ T cell loss: Cytopathic effects, killing by CTLs, pyroptosis
- DC-SIGN on dendritic cells binds HIV envelope and transports virus
- High mutation rate due to error-prone reverse transcriptase → new HIV variants
- Clinical features: Acute HIV disease, clinical latency, AIDS (opportunistic infections, tumors, encephalopathy, wasting syndrome)

**Table 21.7 Clinical Features of HIV Infection:**
| Phase | Clinical Feature |
|---|---|
| Acute HIV disease | Fever, headaches, sore throat with pharyngitis, generalized lymphadenopathy, rashes |
| Clinical latency period | Declining blood CD4+ T cell count |
| AIDS | Opportunistic infections (Protozoa, Bacteria, Fungi, Viruses); Tumors (Lymphomas, Kaposi sarcoma, Cervical carcinoma); Encephalopathy; Wasting syndrome |
